Adam Fistler — Behavioral Change Consultant

My Story, My Struggles, and How They Shaped My Approach

As a Behavioral Change Consultant, I help people explore the patterns that shape how they think, feel, behave, and experience their lives. My work draws from hypnosis, mindfulness, meditation, addiction and recovery, behavioral approaches, psychology, neurochemistry, and systems thinking.

But my interest in these subjects isn't purely academic.

A great deal of what I know about struggle, behavior, and change comes from having lived through some of it myself.

I struggled with extreme shyness and social anxiety from childhood. I spent years feeling disconnected from other people, struggling to understand social situations, and wondering what was wrong with me. Those experiences shaped the way I came to understand behavior, attention, emotion, and the patterns that can develop around them.

Later in life, I developed an addiction and experienced the consequences of it firsthand. My path through addiction, repeated attempts at recovery, homelessness, and eventually rebuilding my life taught me things that books alone could never have taught me.

I've put that story here deliberately and without trying to make it look prettier than it was.

From Personal Experience to a Broader Approach

Those experiences eventually led me to explore many different ways of understanding people and change.

Hypnosis became one of the tools I studied and practiced. Mindfulness and meditation gave me another way to understand attention, awareness, and automatic patterns. My experience with addiction and recovery led me to explore behavioral approaches, motivation, learning, neurochemistry, and the ways that habits can become deeply established.

And my background in technology gave me another perspective that has become central to the way I think.

As a systems engineer and software developer, I learned to look at complicated systems by breaking them into parts, examining how those parts interact, identifying patterns, finding points of failure, and looking for ways to make the system work better.

I don't apply that way of thinking only to computers.

People are systems too.

Our thoughts, emotions, habits, memories, beliefs, environment, relationships, attention, physiology, and behavior interact with one another. Changing one part can affect many others. Sometimes the most useful question isn't "What's wrong with me?" but rather "What is this system doing, and why?"

That perspective is at the center of my approach to personal change and transformation.

I Don't Accept Ideas Simply Because They Sound Interesting

My approach to unconventional subjects has also been shaped by skepticism.

When I became interested in handwriting analysis and graphology, I didn't simply accept every claim associated with them. I studied their history and methods, learned from established practitioners, and experimented with them myself across different people and circumstances. I wanted to see whether something produced sufficiently consistent and useful results before incorporating it into my own repertoire.

I approached astrology in much the same way.

For a long time, I was highly skeptical because the only astrology I knew was the familiar "I'm a Libra, so I must be..." version. I never particularly identified with that description. It was only after learning that a birth chart involves an entire system of planets, signs, houses, aspects, and relationships between them that I began to understand what astrologers were actually working with. I explored its history and structure and tested what I learned against real-world experience.

That doesn't mean I believe every claim made about every system I study.

It means I'm willing to investigate an idea rather than dismissing it simply because it is unfamiliar—and I'm equally willing to reject something when I can't find sufficient consistency or usefulness in it.

That's why I ultimately left numerology out of my repertoire. Despite investigating it, I found too much variation between practitioners and interpretations and too little consistency in my own attempts to apply and test it.

Curiosity without skepticism can become gullibility.
Skepticism without curiosity can become blindness.

I try to practice both.

Why I Tell the Story So Openly

I've been influenced by the work of Brené Brown and by my own experiences with addiction, homelessness, shame, and recovery. One principle that has become important to me is radical transparency.

There are things people often hide because they believe those experiences make them look weak, broken, unsuccessful, or shameful.

I've decided that much of my own story is more useful out in the open.

I don't share it because I think my experiences are unique. I share them because they aren't.

If you've struggled with shyness, social anxiety, addiction, shame, isolation, destructive habits, or simply the feeling that something about your life isn't working the way you want it to, you may recognize parts of yourself in my story.

And if you don't, that's okay too.

You don't have to have lived my life for my experiences to be useful to you.

My hope is simply that something I've learned—through education, experimentation, professional experience, personal failure, recovery, and rebuilding—might give someone another perspective or another possibility to consider.

You don't have to approach change with guilt, shame, or despair.

There are different ways of understanding ourselves. There are different tools for creating change. Sometimes the first step is simply realizing that the way you've been trying to solve a problem isn't the only way to approach it.

That's what I mean by Mind, Meaning & Transformation: understanding the systems and patterns that shape our lives, finding where change is possible, and using that understanding to create something different.
