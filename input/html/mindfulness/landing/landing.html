Mindfulness

Awareness, Attention & the Present Moment

Mindfulness is the practice of deliberately paying attention to what is happening in the present moment. That can include noticing your thoughts, emotions, physical sensations, surroundings, and the way you are responding to what is happening around you.

Mindfulness isn't about emptying your mind or making unpleasant thoughts and emotions disappear. It's about becoming more aware of what is actually happening before automatically reacting to it.

That distinction can be surprisingly important. We often experience a thought, emotion, urge, or physical sensation and immediately respond as though it were an instruction. Mindfulness creates an opportunity to notice the experience first and decide what to do next.

In that sense, mindfulness isn't simply a relaxation technique. It is a way of developing awareness and becoming more deliberate about how we respond to our experiences.

Mindfulness Isn't Just Meditation

Meditation is one way of practicing mindfulness, but mindfulness doesn't require sitting cross-legged in a quiet room for an hour.

You can practice mindfulness while walking, eating, working, listening to someone, washing dishes, exercising, driving, or dealing with a difficult situation.

The essential skill is paying attention to what you are actually experiencing rather than operating entirely on automatic pilot.

A few moments of deliberately noticing your breathing, the sensations in your body, the thoughts passing through your mind, or the emotions you are experiencing can become a mindfulness practice.

Thoughts Are Not Commands

One of the most useful things mindfulness can teach us is that having a thought doesn't mean that we have to believe it or act on it.

Thoughts can appear automatically. Some are useful, some are inaccurate, some are repetitive, and some are simply the mind doing what minds do.

Mindfulness allows us to notice thoughts as events occurring in the mind rather than automatically treating every thought as a fact.

The same principle can apply to emotions, cravings, impulses, and physical sensations. We can learn to notice an experience without immediately turning it into an action.

Mindfulness and Emotional Regulation

Emotions can sometimes seem to arrive before we have had an opportunity to think about them. Anger, anxiety, frustration, fear, excitement, and other emotions can produce powerful physical and behavioral responses.

Mindfulness doesn't require us to suppress those emotions. Instead, it gives us an opportunity to become more aware of what is happening while the emotion is occurring.

What am I feeling? Where do I feel it? What thoughts are accompanying it? What am I about to do? What happens if I act on the impulse? What other choices might I have?

Those questions can create a small but important space between experiencing an emotion and automatically acting on it.

Mindfulness and Automatic Behavior

Much of everyday life happens automatically. We develop routines, habits, expectations, emotional reactions, and familiar ways of responding to particular situations.

Automation is useful. Imagine having to consciously think about every step involved in tying your shoes, brushing your teeth, or walking across a room.

But automatic behavior can also become a problem when a habit continues long after it is useful.

Mindfulness can help bring greater awareness to these patterns. Once we become aware of what we are doing, what we are thinking, and what we are feeling, we have more information with which to decide whether the automatic response is actually serving us.

Mindfulness and Cravings

Cravings provide a particularly useful example of mindfulness in action.

A craving can feel like something that demands an immediate response. Mindfulness provides another possibility: observe the craving.

Notice what triggered it. Notice the thoughts accompanying it. Notice what happens in your body. Notice how the intensity changes over time.

The objective isn't necessarily to make the craving disappear instantly. It is to learn that an urge can be experienced without automatically obeying it.

This can be particularly valuable in recovery, where learning to recognize and respond differently to urges is an important part of developing greater behavioral choice.

Mindfulness and Behavior Change

Lasting behavior change requires more than knowing what we should do.

We can understand perfectly well that a particular behavior is harmful and still find ourselves repeating it. Habits, emotions, environmental cues, expectations, and automatic responses can all influence what we actually do.

Mindfulness can help by increasing awareness of the process that occurs between a situation and our response.

The more clearly we can recognize what is happening, the more opportunities we have to question the automatic response and consider an alternative.

Mindfulness Doesn't Mean You Have to Stop Thinking

One of the most common misconceptions about mindfulness is that you are supposed to stop thinking.

That's not realistic. The human mind produces thoughts. Trying to force yourself not to think can actually become another source of frustration.

Instead, mindfulness involves noticing when your attention has wandered and gently bringing it back to whatever you have chosen to observe.

If you are practicing mindful breathing and suddenly realize that you've been thinking about something that happened three hours ago, you haven't failed at mindfulness.

Noticing that your mind wandered is the practice.

Mindfulness Doesn't Require You to Be Calm

Mindfulness is often associated with peaceful meditation, relaxation, and calmness. Those can certainly be pleasant results of practice, but they aren't requirements.

You can practice mindfulness while angry, anxious, frustrated, excited, bored, or uncomfortable.

In fact, difficult experiences can provide some of the most useful opportunities to practice. Being able to notice what is happening while experiencing a strong emotion is considerably more useful than only being mindful when everything is already going well.

Mindfulness, Meditation & Hypnosis

Mindfulness, meditation, and hypnosis are different practices, but they can overlap in interesting ways.

All three can involve attention, awareness, imagination, expectation, perception, and the way we relate to our internal experiences.

Mindfulness generally emphasizes observing experience with greater awareness and less automatic reaction. Meditation can provide a structured way of developing that ability. Hypnosis can deliberately use focused attention, suggestion, imagery, expectation, and other processes for particular purposes.

Rather than viewing these approaches as competing systems, I find it useful to understand where their techniques overlap and where their purposes differ.

Mindfulness and Recovery

Mindfulness can be particularly useful in recovery because addictive behavior often involves automatic relationships between situations, thoughts, emotions, urges, and behavior.

Learning to recognize those processes can create additional opportunities for choice.

This doesn't mean mindfulness is a cure for addiction, nor does it replace appropriate medical or professional treatment. Instead, it can be one practical skill within a larger recovery toolkit.

The same principle applies more broadly to habits, emotional reactions, stress, and other patterns of behavior.

Learning to Observe Instead of Automatically React

One of the central ideas behind my approach to mindfulness is simple: awareness creates possibilities.

If we don't notice what is happening, we are much more likely to follow the familiar pattern automatically.

When we notice the thought, recognize the emotion, feel the physical sensation, identify the urge, and understand the situation, we have more information available to us.

That doesn't guarantee that we'll always make the perfect decision. It simply gives us a better opportunity to make a deliberate one.

Mindfulness isn't about controlling every thought or emotion. It's about becoming aware enough to have a choice in how you respond.

Mindfulness as a Practical Skill

Mindfulness doesn't have to become another complicated thing you have to add to your life.

It can begin with something as simple as stopping for a moment and noticing what is happening right now.

What am I thinking? What am I feeling? What am I noticing in my body? What am I paying attention to? What am I doing automatically? And, perhaps most importantly, what choices do I have?

With practice, these moments of awareness can become easier to access when they are actually needed — particularly during stress, emotional situations, cravings, conflict, or other circumstances where automatic reactions tend to take over.

Your Practice of Mindfulness

Mindfulness isn't something that has to be mastered before it becomes useful. It is a skill that develops through practice.

The goal isn't to become perfectly calm, perfectly focused, or perfectly aware.

The goal is to become a little more aware of what is happening and a little less controlled by automatic reactions.
